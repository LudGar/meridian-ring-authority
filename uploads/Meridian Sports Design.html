Meridian Sports Design
MRA-01 Meridian · Design sheet · Sports

Games on a spinning floor

Sport on Meridian, from Sunday league football to Grav racing on the underside of the hull. The ring turns once a day, like Earth, and the ballast in its hull holds everyone at 1 g, so the classic sports play exactly as they do at home. What the ring adds is its day: the line between day and night sweeps over the land at 1,309 km/h, and the fastest craft can outrun it.

§1 · The physics every rulebook starts from
24 hone turn of the ring: one day, sunrise to sunrise
1 gfrom the ballast in the hull and walls, not from the spin
0.27 %of a g is all a 24-hour turn makes at a radius of 5,000 km
1,309 km/hspeed of the floor through space, and of the dusk line over the land

A ring this size would have to turn every 75 minutes to make 1 g by spinning, which would mean a 75-minute day. Meridian turns once in 24 hours instead, and 5.4 Moon masses of ballast in the hull and walls make the weight (see the ballast sheet). The spin still exists, it is just too slow to feel: a football kicked 25 m/s spinward lands 1.6 cm short, and a 1,000 km/h craft gets 0.6 % heavier going spinward. No rulebook bothers with it.

What does matter is the day. The sun stands still and the ring turns under it, so the dusk line moves antispinward over the land at 1,309 km/h, the floor's own speed. On Earth's equator it is 1,670 km/h. Anything that flies antispinward faster than that sees the sun climb back up the sky.

§2 · The ballast's field

Gravity comes from below

The ballast lies 6 km under sea level, thickening toward the walls, with more of it in the walls' foot and crest. The result is 0.96 to 1.05 g across all the land, never more than 3° off vertical: ball sports never notice. Away from the land the field changes a lot, and the Grav circuits go exactly there (§8):

  • Inner wall face: 0.31 g at 10 km up, then 3 g by the crest at 24 km.
  • Outer wall face: 2.4 g, pulling sideways into the wall. The face is a floor.
  • Under the hull: 1.03 g, pulling up into the hull. The underside is a second, upside-down ground.
§3 · Venues by settlement

§5 · Venue look, per the CI

The stadium in Meridian's language

Stadiums are built like the spires: a chamfered octagon plan, a bone ceramic bowl banded in slate at every tier, the teal energy line at concourse belt height, and a roof ring that oversails the bowl, glazed at its inner edge. Floodlights are warm white only. Orange marks gates, steward posts and the control frames round the scoreboards, because orange means controls and MRA plant. Pitches use the Surface Kit's paver aprons at their edges, with the kit's lamps and transit stops outside.

§6 · The ring's own sport

Grav racing

Lift-field craft racing a few metres off the ground at up to 2,400 km/h. Grav racing came out of the MRA's own maintenance skiffs, which float on the ballast's field. The circuits run through the parts of the ring the MRA keeps running: the trunk, the walls, the shafts and the hull.

Two things make it Meridian's sport. The first is the field (§2). A craft floats on the ballast's field, so its lift follows the field's map: steady over the land, nearly gone in the light middle of a wall, crushing at the crest, and flipped round on the outer faces and under the hull. Every circuit crosses several of these zones, and the setup is a compromise between them. Reading that compromise is the craft engineer's job, the same tuning loop as the vehicle workshop in the activities sheet. The second is the dusk line (§1). G-2 and G-1 craft fly faster than it, so a long antispinward race can run out of the night into the evening, and a spinward one races into the dark.

§8 · Circuits

Spire GP

Street circuits through a spire city's 90° grid. Roundabouts are the hairpins, the spire plaza is the final chicane, and the finish is under the spire's portals. Every spire city can host one.

G-4 · AS-001 Teskavar GP

Trunk Run

A closed section of the trunk expressway: 31.4 km dead straight from spire to spire, with viaducts and long tunnel stretches. It is a drag race with drafting, decided by energy management.

G-2 · one section per heat

Terrace Slalom

Down the edge terraces: 2 km treads, 140 m sheer faces, gates on the orange edge signals. Every face is a jump.

G-3 · near the walls

Wall Ascent

A vertical climb up the inner face of the rim wall. Checkpoints sit on the ledges every 2 km. Halfway up the field drops to 0.3 g and craft float; the last kilometres under the crest pull 3 g, and that is where the race is won. The air thins and canopies seal. The finish is the teal crest at 24.6 km, then over the lip and down the outer face.

G-1 · 25 km straight up

Spine Drop

Down a spire's 2 km shaft: through the service tunnel, utility gallery, freight hall, depot and rib catwalk to the airlock on L6. Tight, dark and lit only by warm work lights and teal conduits.

G-1 · L0 → L6

Hull Circuit

Out of the airlock and round the outside of the hull, past radiator fields, ribs and docks. Under the hull the ballast pulls up at 1.03 g, so the underside is a track like any other, only upside down, in vacuum, with Saturn under the canopy. Craft come down the outer wall face at 2.4 g and turn the corner onto the underside. Crash here and a recovery drone collects you. The sport's crown race.

G-1 · upside down, in vacuum

Sea Crossing

Low over open water, in the ground-effect band, with spray and swell. Buoy gates in orange.

G-3 · the ring's seas

The Long Dusk

One full lap of the ring, 31,416 km, flown antispinward as a team relay with crew swaps at the metropolises. It starts at sunset in Meridian Central. A team that holds the dusk line exactly needs 24 hours, one whole day spent in a single sunset. Go faster and the sun climbs back up the sky; fall behind and the night catches you. G-1 teams finish in about 16 hours, mostly in afternoon light.

all classes · once a year

Cassini Thread

Not Grav, but the same crowd watches it: shuttles launch from the spaceports, cross 73,000 km to Saturn's rings and thread the Cassini Division, a 4,590 km gap.

ships · off-ring
§9 · Track design language
  1. Bone track bed, slate edges. Track sections are chamfered at 45° and banded in slate, like the buildings, so a track reads as MRA infrastructure and not as an add-on.
  2. Teal rails are energy. They run along the track edge and recharge craft that ride close to them. Teal means energy everywhere else in the CI too, so a player already knows what the rail does.
  3. Orange gates are controls. Gates, sector boards and pit markers are orange. Missing a gate is a penalty.
  4. Warm white light only. Night races are lit warm white from the track edge. The only coloured light is teal energy and orange controls.
  5. Codes on everything. Sector boards carry mono codes in the house style: GB-017 · S3 · SPINWARD. The direction and the local time are printed, because on a long course the time of day changes as you race.
§10 · How a player takes part

Following the activities sheet, sport is a career like any other. A player can do any of these:

  • Compete: sign for a section club or a Grav team and climb the ladder from section to ring.
  • Engineer: tune a craft's lift for a circuit that mixes land, sea and high decks, using telemetry from each surface.
  • Build: design circuits on the real ring with a track editor that places gates, rails and grandstands on actual streets, walls and shafts.
  • Run: own a club, keep a ground (MRA groundskeeping), commentate, or work the recovery drones on the Hull Circuit.
