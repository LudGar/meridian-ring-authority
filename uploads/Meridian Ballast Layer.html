Meridian Ballast Layer
MRA-01 Meridian · Design sheet · Structure

The weight under the floor

Meridian turns once a day, like Earth, so its sun rises and sets every 24 hours. A turn that slow makes almost no gravity, so the ring carries its own: a layer of ultra-dense ballast in the hull and in the rim walls, about 5.4 times the mass of the Moon. This sheet sets out how that layer is shaped and what it does to the world above and around it.

Spin gravity at this size would need a turn every 75 minutes, and with it a 75-minute day. Keeping the 24-hour day means the weight has to come from mass, the way a planet makes it. A wide, dense sheet pulls everything near it onto it, and its pull hardly fades with height as long as the sheet is much wider than you are high. Meridian's floor is 320 km wide, so a sheet under it works up to the tops of the walls.

§2 · A flat sheet is not enough

Why the ballast thickens toward the walls

A sheet of even thickness pulls you toward its middle as well as down. Under a uniform 320 km strip, gravity would lean 20° toward the centre line 80 km out and 43° near the walls, and the whole land would feel like the inside of a bowl. Rivers would run toward the middle and every building near the walls would stand crooked.

The fix has two parts. The hull ballast gets steadily denser toward the walls, and the rim walls carry ballast of their own, a heavy foot in the hull and a heavy crest at the top, which pull back outward. Solved together, that keeps gravity within 4 % of 1 g and within 2.6° of vertical across the whole floor.

§3 · The profile

Hull ballast, centre line to wall

The ballast sits in the hull 6 km below sea level, under everything the spine lifts serve and above the hull's outer skin at 8 km. Under the centre line it carries 2.3 × 10¹⁰ kg per square metre, rising to 6.3 × 10¹⁰ at the wall. As stabilised degenerate matter (10⁹ kg/m³, white-dwarf density) that is a layer 23 m thick at the centre and 63 m at the walls. Each rim wall holds a column of lighter compressed matter, 3 to 30 million kg/m³, densest at its foot and in its top 10 km. The walls carry a quarter of all the ballast.

§4 · The result at sea level
gravity (g), left scale; dashed line = 1 gtilt from vertical (°), right scale

Gravity is lightest on the centre line and in a strip just inside the walls (0.96 g), and heaviest in a band about 120 km out (1.05 g). The tilt never passes 2.6° at sea level or 3.2° on hills 1.5 km high. Nobody standing on the land would notice either. A spirit level would, and MRA survey crews keep a gravity map of every section.

The terraces mark the change. Gravity drops below 1.00 g at 15.4 km from the wall, and the edge terraces begin at 16 km. The two coincide, so the terraces work as the ring's gravity scale. Each step is a band: 0.99 g on the outermost, 0.97 g on the middle steps, then back to 1.01 g on the top step against the wall. The orange edge signal on each terrace face carries its band in mono, T3 · 0.97 g. In the game the HUD shows the local value and terrace step.

The walls light the terraces. A row of warm-white floodlights stands on each wall's first service ledge, 2.1 km up and 400 m apart. At night they light the terraces and foothills: full strength at the foot, half at 5 km, a glow at 15 km. From across the ring they read as one steady line of light along the wall.

§6 · What it changes in the world

The underside is walkable

A dense sheet pulls from both faces. Below the hull you weigh 1.03 g toward it, so the underside, with its radiator fields, ribs and docks, is a real place to walk, drive and build on. It has no air: a vacuum world with Saturn under your feet.

Hull Circuit · hull crews

The outer wall faces are floors

Outside a wall, the wall's ballast pulls you onto its face at 2.4 g. The docks on the outer walls stand on that face. Crews there work heavy, at right angles to everyone inside.

M-08 docks · outer faces

The ring wants to fall in

All that mass pulls the ring toward its own centre at roughly 3 % of a g, and the slow turn offsets less than a tenth of that. The hull works as a compression arch carrying the whole ring's weight. Its compression members are the biggest job the MRA has.

MRA structural maintenance

The ballast must be held

Degenerate matter only stays dense under containment. The ballast decks run teal containment lines, checked by inspection crawlers. A containment slip is the worst accident the ring can have, a raid-scale event in the activities sheet's terms.

§7 · Drawing it in the CI
  1. In cutaways the ballast is graphite. A solid graphite band in the hull with slate hatching, thickening toward the walls as in §3, plus graphite cores in each wall's foot and crest.
  2. Containment is teal. The lines that hold the ballast are energy, so they are teal, like every other energy line on the ring.
  3. Access is orange. Hatches into the ballast decks and their inspection points are MRA plant, so they are orange.
  4. The gravity map is a document. Survey sheets give each section's field in mono codes: S017 · 1.031 g · 0.4° · BAL-OK.
§8 · How the numbers were made

The profile comes from a cross-section of the ring: the floor 320 km wide, walls 25 km high and 4 km thick, the ballast 6 km below sea level. It solves for the non-negative mass layout that gives 1 g straight down at sea level and at 1.5 km, smoothed so it can be built. Three small effects are left out and can be absorbed into the ballast: the rest of the ring pulls about 3 % of a g toward the ring's centre, Saturn's tide is 0.4 %, and the 24-hour spin adds 0.27 % downward.
